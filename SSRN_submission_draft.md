# SSRN Submission — Draft Text

Abstract
Ghana's National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS) was designed to shield households from catastrophic out-of-pocket (OOP) healthcare costs, yet OOP payments still account for approximately 26 percent of current health expenditure nationally — well above the ceiling the World Health Organization recommends (Okoroh and Essoun 2018). This paper asks a generational question that the largely retrospective catastrophic-health-expenditure (CHE) literature does not directly address: if current trends in healthcare cost growth, wage growth, and macroeconomic volatility persist, what OOP burden will Ghana's Generation Z cohort face by the age at which today's CHE literature is typically anchored — roughly 50? We combine two complementary approaches. First, we compute household OOP burden directly from GLSS7 (2016/17) microdata (n = 14,009 households) using an original extraction pipeline, finding a weighted mean burden of 0.75 percent of household expenditure, rising to 0.92–1.04 percent among households aged 60 and older, and a modest but directionally consistent gap by household-head literacy status (0.74 percent for literate-headed households versus 0.84 percent for illiterate-headed households). Second, we construct a stylized Monte Carlo stress-test simulation, calibrated to Ghana's minimum-wage growth series, historical and recent consumer price inflation, national health-financing shares, and CHE incidence from the wider literature — with every parameter classified as Ghana-specific empirical, external empirical, or author-calibrated (Section 3.1) — simulating 10,000 household trajectories under three scenarios: a status-quo continuation of current trends; a financing-reform scenario reaching the Abuja Declaration's 15-percent-of-budget target; and a shock-heavy scenario reflecting Ghana's history of recurrent currency crises. The primary simulation spans 2026–2051 (approximately age 25–50), matching the age at which the CHE literature this paper builds on is typically anchored; under the status-quo scenario, mean household OOP burden rises from approximately 8.1 percent of income in 2026 to 19.6 percent by 2051, and only the financing-reform scenario keeps burden low throughout. We additionally report an extended simulation — the identical model and parameters re-run through 2061 (approximately age 60) — under which status-quo burden reaches 26.7 percent of income and the shock-heavy scenario approaches the entirety of household income; this extension is reported separately from the primary analysis and should not be read as the paper's original design horizon. Because the simulation's catastrophic-event hazard parameter (8 percent annually) was calibrated from wider regional literature rather than this paper's own GLSS7 finding, we additionally back-solve and re-run the primary and extended simulations under a Ghana-empirical calibration anchored to the 1.08 percent estimate; the paper's scenario ordering and policy conclusions are preserved under this alternative, though absolute projected burden is somewhat lower. A one-at-a-time sensitivity analysis of the primary simulation shows the projection is most sensitive to parameters with the strongest empirical grounding (wage growth, currency-shock frequency) and least sensitive to its more speculative assumptions, though we note this design does not capture interaction effects between parameters and report it as a lower bound on total outcome uncertainty. We discuss the structural drivers behind these trajectories — currency exposure in health financing, the breadth-versus-depth problem in NHIS coverage, the epidemiological transition toward non-communicable disease, and rising informal-sector employment — and the specific policy levers available to alter Generation Z's outcome. Throughout, Generation Z is treated as an analytical cohort rather than a homogeneous population, and all simulation outputs — including values approaching or exceeding household income under the shock-heavy scenario — are reported as model-implied outcomes under stated stress-test assumptions, not deterministic predictions of any individual household's experience; full model equations, the parameter-evidence classification, and simulation code are provided to support independent reproduction.
Keywords: catastrophic health expenditure; out-of-pocket payments; National Health Insurance Scheme; Ghana; generational economic projection; Monte Carlo simulation; universal health coverage; GLSS7 microdata

## Title
Healthcare Affordability Burden Study: Ghana's Generation Z

## Author(s)
Nana Owusu Achiaw Prempeh
Academic City University College, Accra, Ghana — MSc Data Science

## Abstract (draft — replace with actual paper abstract)

Rising out-of-pocket healthcare costs pose a growing burden on households in Sub-Saharan Africa, with disproportionate effects on younger cohorts entering the labor market under constrained income conditions. This study quantifies the healthcare affordability burden faced by Generation Z in Ghana using nationally representative microdata from the Ghana Living Standards Survey Round 7 (GLSS7). We construct a household-level affordability burden metric relating out-of-pocket health expenditure to household capacity to pay, and estimate its distribution using a Monte Carlo simulation across a sample of 10,000 households to account for income and expenditure uncertainty. Results are disaggregated by urban and rural residence to identify geographic disparities in affordability. Findings offer evidence relevant to healthcare financing policy and social protection design targeting younger populations in Ghana and comparable Sub-Saharan African contexts.
